# Overshoot saccades: realism

What eyes, their muscles and the brain's control of them do, and what that forces the picture to show. Each heading is a law or a relation. Under it is what follows on screen. What the page simplifies on purpose is said on screen and in the top `README.md`.

## An eye turns about its center

The eye is a ball in a socket. It turns and does not shift.

- The eye's center stays where it is. Only its direction changes.
- For each direction of gaze the eye has one orientation. It does not twist about its line of sight.
- Both eyes aim at the same point on the screen. For a near screen their lines of sight meet there and are not parallel.
- The two eyes move together: at the same time, the same way and by the same amount.

## A saccade's size sets its duration and speed

Measured saccades follow one relation: a bigger jump takes a little longer and peaks faster. A 10° saccade takes about 50 ms.

- No saccade is slow, and none is faster than its size allows.
- Speed rises smoothly to one peak and falls. The eye does not stop partway.
- A saccade is over before the brain can see where it is going. So it cannot be steered in flight.
- A miss is put right by a second saccade, after a wait of more than a tenth of a second. The eye never swerves or creeps back at once.
- Each saccade starts well after the error that causes it, by about an eighth of a second or more.

## Pursuit follows speed and lags

Pursuit is smooth. The eye's speed is a share of the target's speed, called the gain.

- With a gain below one the eye falls behind steadily. The gap grows at the target's speed times what the gain lacks.
- A lower gain needs more catch-up saccades. A faster target does too.
- Pursuit needs a moving target. The eye does not glide across a still screen.
- Pursuit starts about a tenth of a second after the target moves. With a target that swings regularly the eye predicts it, and the delay goes.
- Eye speed cannot change at once. Once a swing is learned, the command leads by as much as the eye is slow to answer, so the eye's speed is the gain times the target's speed at the same moment.
- Healthy pursuit of a learned swing needs no saccade back toward the target at a turn. It needs only a small catch-up saccade now and then, for what the gain lacks.
- What is learned belongs to one swing. When the swing's speed changes, faster or slower, the eye learns it again.

## A saccade is aimed with old news

A saccade is sized from what the eye saw about a tenth of a second before it starts. It cannot use anything later.

- A catch-up saccade is aimed where the target will be. Measured ones land on it or a little short.
- The measured rule assumes the slip stays as it was. That holds for a steady target. For a learned swing the brain knows how the target will slow, and the saccade is sized for the mean slip until it ends.
- So once a swing is learned, a saccade sized by the measured rule never lands ahead of the target, at any speed of the target, with healthy or poor pursuit.
- Before the swing is learned, a saccade made as the target slows for a turn can land a little ahead.

## Ahead and behind are along the target's motion

- Ahead is the way the target is moving. When the target turns round, ahead becomes behind, though neither the eye nor the target jumps.
- A graph of ahead against behind may change only as fast as the eye and the target move. Its only steps are saccades. At a turn the line is broken and the turn is marked.
- A swinging target is never still. At the end of its swing it is turning.

## On the target has a size

Saccades of one size do not all land in one place. Sizes to a still target vary by about 7% from one saccade to the next.

- A saccade counts as on the target when it ends within that share of its own size from the target's middle. The drawn size of the target has no part in it.
- An overshoot saccade starts farther behind the target than that and lands farther ahead. Whether a saccade is one follows from the saccade alone.
- With saccades made a third larger, nearly every catch-up saccade is an overshoot saccade, at any speed of the target. With measured sizes and a learned swing, none is.

## Muscles work in opposed pairs and only pull

Each eye has six muscles in three pairs. A muscle wraps part of the way round the ball to where it is fixed.

- To turn an eye, one muscle of a pair pulls and the other relaxes. During a saccade the partner's nerve signal falls by as much as the puller's rises, and never below zero. So it stops in all but small, slow saccades.
- For a look to the right, the outer muscle of the right eye and the inner muscle of the left eye pull together.
- The pulling muscle's signal has a burst that goes with speed and a steady part that goes with position. The burst lasts as long as the saccade.
- A muscle's length changes by the eye's radius times the angle turned: about 2 mm for 10°.
- A muscle gets thicker as it shortens, because its volume stays the same. Its thickness does not follow its nerve signal.
- A muscle may be drawn brighter when its signal is higher. Its outline must not change with brightness, or it looks thicker when only the signal has changed.
- A muscle lies on the ball and never enters it. Where it is fixed moves with the ball.
- A muscle's middle line has no step in it. Where its straight run meets the ball it goes on smoothly.
- A muscle can only pull against something it is fixed to. Its back end is fixed to bone, and the loop of the superior oblique is fixed to bone. A picture that leaves the bone out says so.

## Two muscles never share space

Where two muscles cross, one lies on the ball and the other rides over it for the whole crossing.

- The tendon of the superior oblique runs under the superior rectus.
- The inferior oblique passes outside the inferior rectus, between it and the floor of the eye socket. It ends under the lateral rectus.
- The order is the same at every direction of gaze. A muscle does not pass through another on the way from one side of it to the other.
- A tendon cannot hang in the air above a muscle and then pass down through it. A tight muscle holds down what runs beneath it.
- Riding over another muscle does not change a muscle's length. Its length is that of its path.
- The lift of a rider changes smoothly as the eye turns. Nothing jumps.

## What is seen follows where the eye points

- The middle of the eye's view is where its line of sight meets the screen.
- When the eye turns one way, the picture in its view moves the other way by the same angle.
- The trace of eye against target, the ticks on the screen and the picture all show the same moment.
- A mark of a past event stays on the screen as long as the same event stays on the trace.
- When time is slowed, the target, the eyes, the muscles and the trace are slowed alike.
- Nothing drawn for explanation covers the target or the place the eyes look. The target is drawn last. The mark for where the eyes look is a ring wider than the target.
- A real screen shows only the target. Marks an eye tracker would add are named as such.
- A degree at the screen has a size: about 10.5 mm at 60 cm. The target is 0.8° across.
- Both eyes are in view wherever muscles are shown, so that the muscles that pull together can be compared.

## Looks wrong, is right

- With accurate saccades the eye often lands a little short of a moving target. Measured saccades do.
- The opposing muscle goes quiet during a saccade.
- Poor pursuit alone does not make saccades overshoot. It makes more saccades.
- The trace has a gap and a thin line at each turn of the target, with the line ahead of the target on one side and behind it on the other. The eye did not jump. Ahead changed sides.
- With the overshoot setting and healthy pursuit, every saccade is an overshoot saccade. There are few of them.
- With poor pursuit a saccade can go against the target's motion just after a turn. The eye was behind, the target turned, and the eye is now ahead.
- The superior rectus and the inferior oblique stand a few millimeters off the ball where they cross another muscle. The muscle beneath is that thick.
- The tendon of the superior oblique meets the ball before it reaches the superior rectus, and is not tangent to the ball there. The rectus holds it down.
- For one swing after the target's speed changes, an accurate saccade made near a turn can land a little ahead. The eyes have not learned the new swing yet.
- In the trace the line can rise just before a turn with no saccade. Before a swing is learned the eye runs on at the target's earlier speed while the target slows.
