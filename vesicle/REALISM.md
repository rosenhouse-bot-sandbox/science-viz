# Vesicle fusion: realism

What membranes, molecules and a microscope do, and what that forces the picture to show. Each heading is a law or a relation. Under it is what follows on screen. What the page simplifies on purpose is said on screen and in the top `README.md`.

## A membrane is two sheets of lipid, and lipid is conserved

Each sheet is one molecule thick. Lipids move sideways within their own sheet and do not hop to the other one. None is made or lost during fusion.

- The membrane keeps its thickness as it bends. It has no gaps and no doubled patches, at any stage. Where the
  vesicle's area pushes the cell's sheet out from the axis, the sheet stays evenly packed: it does not open into
  spokes or wedges at the foot of the dome.
- The vesicle's area is added to the cell membrane. It does not vanish.
- In hemifusion only the two sheets that face each other have joined. The far sheets are still separate, and nothing leaks.
- Lipids of the two membranes mix sheet by sheet: the near sheets first, the far sheets only once the pore is open.
- The inside of the vesicle becomes the outside of the cell.
- The vesicle has flattened into the membrane by the time the words say it has.

## A membrane protein stays in its membrane, the same way up

A protein that spans the membrane cannot leave it or turn over in it.

- The end that faced the cytoplasm still faces it after fusion. The end that faced the inside of the vesicle now faces the cleft.
- A protein goes from the vesicle to the cell membrane only through the neck, and only once the neck is wide enough for its bulk.
- No protein crosses the cytoplasm or the inside of the vesicle, and none is pushed into a membrane.
- The tags inside the vesicle end up outside the cell.

## A protein keeps its length and number

A protein is a chain of fixed length, and a vesicle carries a counted number of each kind.

- A tether's link is the same length in every frame. It is coiled when the vesicle is near and straight when the
  vesicle pulls it tight. It binds the vesicle only when its site is within that length, holds it no farther, and
  stays on its rod when it releases the vesicle. Measured tethers are shorter than 40 nm, so rod and link together are too, and
  the vesicle is caught only that close to the membrane.
- Each kind of protein has the same count in every frame and in both views, because it is the same vesicle: about
  70 synaptobrevins and 15 synaptotagmins.

## Things this small move at random

Heat keeps every molecule and every vesicle moving at random, as far in one direction as in another. A free body also tumbles.

- A vesicle that is not held wanders up, down and sideways alike, and turns as it goes. Nothing steers it to the
  membrane. Its path is one random walk with the same steps in all three directions, and it ends where the
  tethers can reach.
- A free vesicle tumbles as one body. Everything on it turns with it, the SNARE end too, until something holds it.
- A vesicle that is held by tethers moves less. A docked one hardly moves.
- Glutamate leaves only through the open pore, by random motion. None leaves before the pore opens.
- A glutamate molecule crosses the vesicle many times before it finds the pore. So the inside thins evenly. It does not empty from one end.
- Diffusion runs down a concentration. Nothing pulls glutamate to the pore: there is no stream or funnel inside, and
  the pore holds no more glutamate than its width holds at the lumen's concentration. A narrow pore lets it out slowly.
- The count inside plus the count outside stays the same. A molecule bound to a receptor is one of those released.
- Outside, glutamate spreads from the pore's mouth as a round cloud, as far across the cleft as along it. It is
  not a stream that crosses and then spreads along the far membrane. It thins as it spreads. A dot keeps its size.
- A receptor shows glutamate only once drawn glutamate has reached it. An AMPA receptor opens with two bound and
  releases them a few milliseconds later. An NMDA receptor binds two glutamates.

## Everything runs on one clock

The slider is not a clock. The stages differ a thousand times in length, so the slider gives each its own pace.
Within a stage everything that moves at random has had the same time, and each goes as far as its diffusion
coefficient allows: glutamate in the cleft about 0.33 µm²/ms, a lipid in a membrane about 5 µm²/s, a vesicle
protein about 0.2 µm²/s.

- In every frame glutamate has spread farthest, the lipids next, and the proteins least.
- A protein in a membrane goes less far than the lipids around it. The vesicle's lipids are mixed into the cell
  membrane while its proteins are still near the site.
- Glutamate leaves the drawn patch during the Release stage. It is gone long before the vesicle has flattened.
- Other vesicles in the field are on the same clock as ours.
- A sentence that gives a time says which stage it is for.

## Proteins do the pulling

The membranes do not come together by themselves. The SNARE proteins join at the ends far from the membranes and wind toward them.

- Synaptobrevin is anchored in the vesicle. Syntaxin and SNAP-25 are anchored in the cell membrane.
- The membranes are pulled together where the SNAREs are. Winding comes before hemifusion, and hemifusion before the pore.
- Calcium comes first. The channels open when the nerve impulse arrives, and not before. Until calcium has bound
  synaptotagmin, the gap between the membranes, the neck and the winding of the SNAREs do not change. Then the
  winding closes the gap.

## Total reflection leaves a thin lit layer

Past the critical angle all the laser's light reflects in the glass. A field still reaches a short way into the cell and dies away with height, to about a third in every 100 nm or so.

- Below the critical angle the beam goes on into the cell and lights everything.
- The lit layer is deepest just past the critical angle and gets thinner as the angle grows.
- The field at the glass depends on the angle too: past the critical angle it is `4 cos²θ / (1 − n²)` of the beam.
  So a steeper beam lights a thinner layer, and lights it less.
- A tag's brightness falls steeply with its height above the glass. A vesicle that is not yet at the membrane is dim.
- The reflected beam leaves at the angle it came in.
- The lens cannot send or collect light steeper than its own cone.

## The tag is dark in acid

The inside of a vesicle is acidic, and the tag is nearly dark there. Protons are small and leave at once when the pore opens.

- The spot brightens when the pore opens, not before.
- After fusion the tags spread sideways in the cell membrane. The spot widens and its peak falls, while its total holds.
- The two proteins of one tag sit on one synaptobrevin, so the two colors of a spot spread and fade together.
- A picture and its trace come from the same molecules: the trace sums the tags that the camera picture draws.
- A readout of our vesicle is its light alone. Docked, the 510 nm light is a few photons and the 589 nm light about
  half its value outside. At the pore the 510 nm light rises about 25 times and the 589 nm light doubles.

## A microscope cannot draw anything smaller than its blur

A point of light is recorded as a spot about half a wavelength across, divided by the lens's aperture. That is about 175 nm here. The vesicle is 42 nm.

- The camera's spot is several times wider than the vesicle and does not show its shape.
- Longer wavelengths give a wider spot.
- Light arrives as single photons. A pixel that expects N photons varies by about the square root of N, so a dim picture is a noisy one.
- In the reflection picture, brightness goes by height above the glass, and repeats every half wavelength in the cell. A dark or bright spot there is a vesicle near the membrane, not a dent in it.
- Out of focus, a spot is wider and its peak is lower. One objective serves every picture, and each wavelength
  focuses at its own depth, so focusing for one picture can sharpen another and blur a third.

## A stated range holds the truth as often as it says

- A 90% range misses the truth about one time in ten, and narrows with more light.
- It is taken from the belief given the frames shown, never from the sampler's walk in to that belief. Until the
  walkers have arrived, no range is shown, and the page says the numbers are not ready.
- More light cannot widen a range.

## Looks wrong, is right

- The fluorescent spot is much bigger than the vesicle.
- The reserve vesicles do not wander. They are tethered.
- A bulky protein stays on the vesicle after smaller ones have left. The neck is not yet wide enough for it.
- Glutamate is gone from the picture early in the Release stage, while the vesicle is still round. It moves
  about a thousand times faster than a protein in a membrane.
- By the Collapse stage the vesicle's lipid color has gone from the patch, while its proteins are still near the
  site. The lipids have spread beyond the patch; the proteins are slower.
- The pore is open for a while before much glutamate comes out. While its opening is barely wider than a
  glutamate molecule, almost nothing passes.
- The tethers are slack once the vesicle is docked. They held it on the way in; the SNAREs hold it now.
- The pink balls on the cytoplasmic face of the membrane after the pore opens are synaptophysin, not glutamate.
- The vesicle holds about 1,500 glutamate molecules at 150 mM, a little under the 1,790 counted for a vesicle of the
  same size, because the wall is drawn thicker and the lumen is smaller.
- The picture is dimmer at 79° than at 68°, even on the glass. The field at the surface is weaker at a steeper angle.
- With the reflection camera at 630 nm and the focus given to it, the 589 nm picture gets sharper and the 510 nm
  picture blurs. The 589 nm light focuses deeper than the 510 nm light, near where the 630 nm light does.
- Before the pore opens, the brightest pixels near our vesicle are other vesicles. Ours gives only a few photons at
  510 nm. Its readout is a count less what the rest of the field is expected to give there, so with noise it can
  dip just below zero.
