# Rainbows: realism

What light does in rain, and what that forces the picture to show. Each heading is a law or a relation. Under it is what follows on screen. What the page simplifies on purpose is said on screen and in the top `README.md`.

## A drop sends light back in a cone

Sunlight arrives as parallel rays. A ray that enters a round drop, reflects once inside and leaves is turned by at least 138°. Many rays leave close to that angle. So a drop looks bright when it is 42° from the line that runs from the Sun through the eye.

- The bow is a circle 42° from the point opposite the Sun. The shadow of the viewer's head falls on that point.
- A bow belongs to an eye, not to a place. If the eye moves, the bow moves with it.
- The top of the bow stands 42° less the Sun's height above the horizon. With the Sun higher than 42° there is no main bow over flat ground.
- A falling drop is in the bow only for a moment. At the top of the bow it is dark, then it sends red, then the rest in order to violet, then a faint white.
- After one reflection no light comes back beyond 42°. So the sky is brighter inside the bow than outside it.
- Two reflections make a second bow near 51°. It is fainter, and its order is reversed. The sky between the two bows is the darkest.
- The second bow's top stands 51° less the Sun's height. So with the Sun between 42° and 51° up, the outer bow is still there, and words that say "no bow" are false.
- Light goes from the Sun to the drop to the eye. No ray starts at the eye.

## Two reflections make a second fold

Light that reflects twice inside the drop is turned least near 51°, and piles up there as the first bow's light piles up near 42°.

- A view that follows two reflections draws the rays that leave near 51°, chosen as the rays to the eye are. They are fainter than the first bow's, with red on the inside, and they are no more in number than the first bow's set.
- None of that light reaches an eye in the first bow. A drop sends it to the eye only when the drop sits near 51°, and a falling drop that passes 51° is colored by the outer bow.

## The light reaching the eye changes smoothly with the eye's place

The Sun is a disc 0.53° across. A ray counts by how much of the disc could have sent it, which falls to nothing at the edge of the band of directions that reach the eye.

- Each ray to the eye is drawn by that weight. As the drop moves, a ray fades in from nothing and out to nothing. No ray appears or vanishes at full strength, and the bundle's brightness follows the light that reaches the eye, not the number of rays drawn.

## Light bends by Snell's law and reflects at an equal angle

- The whole path of a ray lies in one plane through the drop's center.
- Inside a round drop the ray meets the wall at the same angle every time.
- That angle is below the critical angle, so the light is never totally reflected. Most of it leaves at each meeting. The bow is made by the small share that reflected.
- At a flat patch of surface there are three beams: arriving, reflected and passing.

## Shorter wavelengths bend more

Water's index is higher for shorter wavelengths.

- The ray we see as violet leaves at 40.6° and the one we see as red at 42.4°. Red is on the outside of the first bow, and the bow is about 2° wide.
- A ray keeps its wavelength through air and water, so the color drawn for it does not change along the path. That holds however bright it is drawn: light too bright for the screen is dimmed with no change of hue, or bright red over blue water would turn pink-white and bright violet white.
- The color drawn is the color of the wavelength set. While the wavelength moves, the color drawn catches up within about a quarter of a second, at a steady rate through the colors, with no burst where hue changes fastest (near 490 and 590 nm).
- With the colors drawn spread out 5×, the angles and wavelengths given are still the real ones.
- Sunlight is a mix of wavelengths. They come apart only because each leaves the drop at its own angle.
- A drop also sends each wavelength inside that wavelength's own bow angle. So a drop in the bow sends a mix, seen paler and redder than the wavelength whose bow is there: a drop at the 455 nm angle is seen pale blue, not indigo. A drop is named by the color it is drawn in, and the color comes from that mix.

## Light is conserved at a surface

The shares reflected and passed at a surface add up to what arrived.

- One photon in is one photon out. The counts on each path follow the shares.
- A beam's drawn brightness follows the power it carries per unit of width (its irradiance). The reflected and passed beams, each times the width of that beam, add up to the arriving one. Straight on (0°), s and p are the same light and are drawn alike, and the beam leaving carries less light than arrived. Drawing the square of the field would not do: in water E is weaker and the magnetic field stronger than in air for the same power, by about n.
- Shares over the whole drop are averages over its face, each impact parameter weighted by the ring of the face it stands for. About 7% reflects off the front, 88% leaves through the back, 4% leaves after one reflection and 0.6% after two. The rainbow ray's own shares differ (6%, 89%, 4.6% and 0.5%), and a number says which it is.
- The bow's light is strongly polarized. The reflection inside is close to the angle at which one polarization does not reflect. About 96% of the first bow's light vibrates along the bow. Its degree of polarization, (Is − Ip)/(Is + Ip), is about 93%: the two numbers are different things.

## Light is slower in water

Light travels at c in air and c/1.33 in water. Its frequency is the same on both sides.

- Crests are 1.33 times closer together in water, and they advance 1.33 times slower.
- Crests on the two sides of a surface meet along it without a break. That is why the ray bends.
- Dots of light inside the drop move a quarter slower than outside and sit closer together.
- Changing the wavelength changes the crest spacing in proportion.

## Why water slows light

The light's electric field pushes the electrons in each molecule. They are driven far below the frequency at which they would resonate.

- Electrons move in step with the field. The nuclei hardly move.
- An electron's swing is in proportion to the field where it is. In the water that field is t times the arriving one, so the swing shrinks as the light comes in more steeply.
- Each layer of molecules sends out its own wave, a quarter cycle behind. Added to the arriving wave, it makes the total fall behind.
- Shorter wavelengths are nearer the resonance, so the electrons move farther and the light is slowed more. This is the bending above.
- Liquid water has about 33 molecules in a cubic nanometer. Air has almost none at that scale.

## The scales are one world

- The Sun is in the same direction at Orbit, Rain and Drop.
- The drop at Drop is one of the drops of Rain, in its place. The patch at Surface is a patch of that drop, and the molecules are in that patch.
- Light's wavelength is about two thousand times smaller than a raindrop and more than a thousand times larger than a molecule. A picture with a few crests across a drop, or a crest among a few molecules, is not to scale and says so.
- The air and its weather are a layer a few thousandths of the Earth's radius thick.
- Rain falls straight down in still air at a steady few meters a second. Small drops are round.
- A drop's fall speed is set by its size: about 4 m/s at 1 mm across, 6.5 m/s at 2 mm and 8 m/s at 3 mm, measured in still air. A slowdown is quoted for the size of drop that is drawn, here the Drop scale's 2 mm.
- A drop falls on to the ground. It does not fade out in mid-air or appear from nothing. A marked drop comes into view at the top of the rain, as each raindrop there does, and ends on the ground.
- Light keeps its polarization from scale to scale. The light at Molecules is polarized as Surface was set: with s, E points across the plane of the bend; with p, it lies in that plane.
- The eye at Drop is drawn a few drop widths away, and in truth it is meters away. The page says so under the picture.
- The air is drawn far thicker than it is around the Earth, and the page says so.
- Rain falls from cloud, and the Sun's side of the sky is clear.
- Clouds are carried with the ground under them. They do not drift over a globe that is not turning.
- If rain is slowed so that it can be followed, everything in the scene is slowed alike, or the page says what is not.

## Looks wrong, is right

- Light leaves the drop at every meeting with the wall. Nothing is trapped inside.
- The second bow's order is the reverse of the first's.
- Entering at a grazing angle, the beam in the water is much wider than the beam in air (7.6 times at 85°) and dimmer per unit of width. Leaving near the critical angle, the beam in air is narrower (0.27 times at 47.5°) and so brighter per unit of width, though it carries less light than arrived.
- The Surface slider stops at 47.5° for light inside the water. Beyond the critical angle (48.6° at 550 nm) nothing passes, and light inside a sphere never meets the wall that steeply.
- Over the blue water a red beam looks pink. That is red light added to the water's blue, not a change of wavelength.
- 450 nm is drawn violet-blue, and 615 to 700 nm a crimson with a little blue. These are the colors of those wavelengths, moved toward white just far enough for a screen to show them.
- The band between the two bows is only a little darker than the sky outside the outer bow. Both send a few hundredths of the light of the bow itself, so against the sky behind the rain the band hardly shows.
- With the Sun between 42° and 51° up, only the faint outer bow is left.
- Water's index is given relative to air. It agrees with measured values for water at 20 °C, divided by air's 1.0003, to 0.0004.
- Zooming from the Drop scale to Surface sets the wavelength to the one the drop sends the eye from where it sits. The Drop readout gives that wavelength, and the Surface slider shows it.
