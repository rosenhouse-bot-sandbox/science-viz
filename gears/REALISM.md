# Gear fidget: realism

What meshing gears and machine tools do, and what that forces the pictures to show. Each heading is a law or a relation. Under it is what follows on screen. What the pages simplify on purpose is said on screen and in the top `README.md`.

## Meshing gears roll on each other without slipping

Where two gears touch, their teeth move at the same speed. So two meshing gears turn opposite ways, at speeds in the inverse ratio of their tooth counts.

- Equal gears turn at equal speeds, each opposite to its neighbors.
- A closed ring of meshing gears can turn only if it has an even count. Three gears round a corner of a cube jam. Four round a corner of an octahedron turn.
- Turning one gear turns all the others. No gear turns alone, and none stays still while its neighbor turns.
- A tooth of one gear sits in a gap of the other. This holds at every contact at once, all the way round every ring.
- A tooth never enters another tooth. The gap left between them is small: a gear held still lets its neighbor rock only slightly.

## A gear is driven only by a flank that touches it

A tooth pushes only where it touches. A maker's standard sets the gap between meshing teeth, the backlash, for each module and size.

- The backlash is within JIS B 1705 grade 0. Each tooth is thinned by the middle of that range: 0.054 mm on the cube, 0.050 mm on the octahedron. A pair has twice that.
- The gear that is turned drives. Each driven tooth rests on the flank that pushes it, and all its backlash is on the other side.
- Each mesh further from the turned gear lags by one more backlash.
- When the turn changes way, the driven gears stand still while the driver crosses the backlash. Then they follow, the nearest first.
- Turning another gear makes that gear the driver. The others stay where they are until a tooth pushes them.

## Bevel gears share one point

The axes of all the gears meet at the center of the solid. Each gear's teeth lie on a cone with its point there.

- Neighboring cones touch along a line through that point. Their half-angles add up to the angle between the axes.
- Teeth get narrower and shallower toward the point.
- Each axis goes through the middle of a face, square to it.

## The parts are solid and are held together

- No part passes through another. The core is clear of every gear.
- A gear turns on its bearing. The bearing's inner ring is held by the screw, and the outer ring turns with the gear.
- A bearing's balls go round at about half the gear's speed, a little more here.
- A screw goes into a threaded hole. Every screw has its hole, and every hole is where its screw is.
- A bevel gear's teeth push it outward along its axis. The gear holds the bearing's outer ring and the screw clamps the inner ring, so the bearing takes that push.
- The screw's boss presses the bearing's inner ring onto the spacer and the spacer onto the core. The screw's shoulder is a little shorter than the two, so its end touches nothing.
- Taken apart, each part moves out along its own axis, in the order it was put on.
- Gears that are apart do not mesh, so one does not turn another.

## A screw moves along its axis only by turning

A thread is a helix. A screw goes one pitch for each turn.

- The screw's thread and the hole's are right-hand helices of the same pitch, 0.3 mm for M1.4, and they are in step.
- Taken apart, a screw turns counterclockwise seen from its head, one turn for each 0.3 mm it comes out. Its 2.5 mm of thread take 8.3 turns. Once the thread has left the hole, the screw stops turning.

## A spin is seen from a side

Which way a wheel turns depends on the side it is seen from.

- Gold and blue are the way each gear turns seen from outside the solid, each from its own side. Neighbors are always one of each.
- Two gold gears on opposite faces of the cube turn opposite ways in space, as the two side gears of a differential do.
- Gears that are apart do not turn, so the key is not shown then.

## A thing at rest is held by what it touches

- What a toy rests on does not turn, or the toy rolls along it.
- Parts drawn apart in the air are an exploded view.

## A solid is there from every side

- The desk is there whichever way the view turns. The view stays above the desk, so the toy is never seen through it.

## A bought part has its maker's sizes

A part that is bought is named as its maker names it, and has the sizes in the maker's catalog.

- The bearing is 1.5 × 4 × 2 mm. In the catalog that is the shielded 681XZZ, with seven 0.6 mm balls. The open 681X is 1.2 mm wide.
- A shielded bearing shows a steel ring on each side, fixed in the outer ring. Its balls are behind them.
- A reamed H7 bore does not grip a bearing. The bearing is held in the gear with retaining compound.

## Machining only removes metal

A part starts as stock that is bigger than it everywhere. Metal comes off only where a cutting edge passes.

- The part at each step is the part at the step before, less what the tool swept through.
- A cut surface appears behind the tool as it moves, not all at once. Chips leave from the cut while it is being made, and at no other time.
- A hole, a groove or a flat stays on every later step.
- The finished part is the part in the drawing, size for size.
- Every pass takes metal off. A pass through air is not in the plan.
- The chips are the metal removed. They come from where the edge is in the metal, and a chip is as wide as the cut is deep. The pile stays for as long as the machine and what holds the work on it stay.

## A round cutter cuts on its whole arc

A cutter's rim is a circle. The rim meets the work before the cutter's lowest point does.

- A pass starts with the whole arc clear of the metal, and ends when the lowest point is past the far end.
- Ahead of the lowest point the cut rises along the arc. Behind it the cut is at full depth.
- A slitting saw or a gear cutter is in the work before the cutter's center is over it.
- A form cutter has one shape, so the gap it leaves has that shape all along. The cutter is as wide as its cut.
- A bevel tooth's shape changes along its length. So the cutter is no wider than the gap's narrowest place, and a pass each side takes the rest. One cutter cannot make the whole flank. The picture draws the flank finished and says how far that is past the cutter.

## A thread is one pitch a turn

- A tap or a die goes one pitch further for each turn. It comes out by the same turns, backward.
- A small tap or die is turned by hand. The spindle is not under power.

## A pass lasts as long as travel divided by feed

- Machine time is the tool's travel, as the picture moves it, divided by the feed.
- The picture has one clock. It plays machine time faster than life. The spindle is drawn slower than its stated speed, by the same factor in every step, and the page says so.
- When the time is paused nothing moves, the spindle too.

## Only a cutting edge touches the work, and only the work

- The tool's holder, the post, the chuck, its jaws, the vise and the spindle touch nothing they are not bolted or clamped to.
- A tool comes to the work and leaves it through open space. Between passes it backs out, returns and steps in.
- The work is held by a surface that is not being cut. A cut stops short of the jaws.
- What was held is cut in a second setup, with the part turned round and held by a finished surface.
- A tool fits where it goes. A boring bar is thinner than the hole it bores.
- A tool is the size of its cut. A center drill has the shape of the hole it leaves.
- A mandrel ends inside the bore it holds, so a tool that faces across the bore's end meets only the part.
- A tool is held too. A boring bar's holder lies in the tool post's slot.

## The work is held against the cut

What holds the work touches it over an area, and that area is there until the last cut that needs it.

- A vise's jaws lie flat on two parallel faces. A collet's bore is the size it grips, and its face is where the grip ends. Each is drawn.
- A cut that would remove the surface a later cut is held by comes after that cut, or the part gets a fixture.
- A block cut back on every edge has no two parallel faces left. The octahedron's core is cut on four edges in the vise and on the rest in the indexer.
- When one end is finished and nothing square is left, the part is screwed to a V block by its own tapped holes. The faces that sit in the V are finished first.
- A gear on a mandrel is clamped against the mandrel's shoulder. The clamp is inside the gear's recess, below the cutter's path.
- A short ring in a collet sits against a stop.

## Within a setup everything moves; between setups the part is moved by hand

- A slide, the tailstock, the indexer and the head get from one place to the next by moving there. Nothing jumps.
- A tool is changed when it is clear of the work.
- A taper is turned with the compound: its top slide carries the tool along the set angle, and the carriage and the cross slide stand still.
- On a knee mill the table carries the work under a spindle that stays where it is. In the picture the spindle stands still and the vise, the indexer or the dividing head slides under it. The quill moves only along its own axis.
- Taking the part out and setting it up again is done by hand and is not shown. The picture changes once, when the setup's words do, after the last tool has pulled back.
- The words name what is in the picture: the setup, the step, and the tool in the spindle or the post with its speed.

## One thing spins

On a lathe the work spins and the tool is fed slowly. On a mill the tool spins and the work is fed.

- A lathe tool's tip is at the height of the work's axis. A drill in a lathe is on that axis.
- The spindle turns the way that brings metal onto the cutting edge, not onto its back. A cutter's teeth and a drill's flutes are made for one way round.
- A drill in the lathe's tailstock does not turn. Its flutes stand still and the work turns.
- The tool and the metal set how fast the edge may go through the metal. So a small drill spins fast and a wide cutter slowly.
- Gear teeth are cut one gap at a time. The blank is turned by one tooth's share of a circle between gaps and is held still during a cut.

## A cut is as deep as the machine, the tool and the part can stand

Small machines and small brass parts take light cuts.

- Roughing passes are fractions of a millimeter deep. A finishing pass is a few hundredths.
- A deep feature is reached in several passes. A deep hole is drilled in pecks, with the drill drawn back to clear chips.
- Roughing comes before finishing. The part is cut from the bar last.

## Surfaces show how they were made and how they have been kept

How sharp a reflection is depends on how smooth the surface is.

- Freshly cut brass is bright. Bar stock is duller.
- A turned surface has fine rings round its axis. A face cut by a fly cutter, or by the bottom of an end mill, has arcs. A face cut by a cutter's side has neither.
- On a gear the faces, the bore, the back cone and the tooth tops are turned and carry the rings. The flanks and the gap floors are cut by the form cutter and do not.
- A machine is painted cast iron and steel that has been used. Its reflections are broad and dull. Nothing on it is a mirror.

## Lights stay in the room

A light stays where it is when the viewer moves, so reflections slide over the metal as the view turns.

- The lit look's room is fixed to the world. The plain look's studio lights are too: they are where the camera sees them from the opening view.

## Looks wrong, is right

- A cube carries gears on only four faces. Six would jam.
- The balls in a bearing go round slower than the gear.
- The gear cutter starts well outside the blank. At full depth the rim reaches about 9 mm ahead of the lowest point.
- The octahedron's core is held for its last cuts by two small screws. The V block takes the load: those cuts push the part into the V.
- While the tailstock drills, the tool post stands back from the work, out toward the operator.
- The four wide flats on the octahedron's block get narrow later. The faces cut most of each away.
- A driven gear stands still for a moment when the turn changes way. That is the backlash.
- Each tooth touches its neighbor on one side only, with a thin gap on the other.
- A screw spins fast while the set comes apart: over eight turns in its first 2.5 mm.
- A gear's wide flat face points outward and its teeth slope back toward the core. The cones' point is at the center of the solid, so the large end of each bevel gear is outside.
- The teeth's inner ends hang below the gear's flat back and over the core's edges. The core's edges are cut back, and the test finds no contact.
- The picture repeats after one tooth spacing. All teeth of a gear are the same.
- The screw's slot and the bearing's inner ring stay still while the gear turns. The screw clamps the inner ring and the spacer to the core.
- At full take-apart the screw is still through the bearing. The parts stay in order along the axis.
- In the opening view the assembled toy seems to cast no shadow. The light is over the viewer's shoulder, so the shadow falls behind the toy.
- The octahedron's core takes about three times as long as the cube's. About eight times as much metal comes off it.
